# Relationship Cognitive Ontology (RCO)

## Relational reality: Agency, environment, and cognition

*A proposed philosophical and computational framework for understanding reality through relationships between agents, their environments, and the cognitive processes through which they experience, interpret, and influence existence.*

## 1. Reality as relationship

Reality can be understood not merely as a collection of independently existing things, but as a system of relationships between things that exist, perceive, interact, and change.

An **agent** receives information, maintains state, and responds to conditions. **Agency** is the capacity to initiate, influence, or regulate change. An **environment** is the context of entities, conditions, resources, information, and possibilities within which agency operates.

The agent–environment distinction is relational rather than absolute: a person inhabits a society, a cell inhabits an organism, and an organism inhabits an ecosystem. An entity may be an agent in one relationship and part of an environment in another.

Four useful dimensions of reality are:

| Dimension | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Objective | Conditions independent of a particular agent's beliefs |
| Subjective | Conditions as experienced and interpreted by an agent |
| Intersubjective | Understandings established and maintained between agents |
| Relational | Interactions, dependencies, and possibilities among entities |

These dimensions coexist. An experience can be real as an experience without every interpretation of it corresponding to external conditions.

## 2. The agent–environment relationship

An agent is both a product of its environment and a contributor to it. A recurring process is: environmental condition → perception → interpretation → valuation → decision → action → environmental feedback → adaptation.

A conceptual model is:

\[R_a(t)=\mathcal{R}(A_t,E_t,H_a)\]

Here \(R_a\) is the agent's experienced relational reality, \(A_t\) its state, \(E_t\) its environment's state, and \(H_a\) its interaction history. Two agents in the same environment can experience different affordances: a tree affords shelter to a bird, shade to a person, and nutrients to a fungus.

## 3. NTFS: Four complementary cognitive functions

Inspired by Jung's psychological functions, the model treats **iNtuition (N)** as possibility and inference, **Thinking (T)** as coherence and reasoning, **Feeling (F)** as value and significance, and **Sensing (S)** as actuality and observation.

N and S primarily concern perception; T and F primarily concern evaluation. Feeling here means valuation, not simply emotion; thinking does not mean intelligence itself.

Each function has an inward and outward orientation. **Introverted** indicates internal representations, experiences, or principles as the primary reference; **extraverted** indicates external conditions, objects, and relationships as the primary reference. These are reference orientations, not exclusive directions of information flow.

| Function | Introverted | Extraverted |
|---|---|---|
| N | Ni: synthesis of internal patterns and trajectories | Ne: exploration of environmental possibilities |
| T | Ti: internal coherence, definitions, contradiction checking | Te: external verification, organization, and effectiveness |
| F | Fi: internal preferences, commitments, and significance | Fe: relational values, coordination, and shared priorities |
| S | Si: subjective sensation, familiarity, experiential continuity | Se: present observable conditions and environmental change |

## 4. Cognitive encompassing

Cognitive encompassing is the integration of these complementary modes without collapsing their distinctions:

- Ni ↔ Ne: synthesis and exploration of possibilities.
- Ti ↔ Te: logical explanation and empirical testing.
- Fi ↔ Fe: individual and relational valuation.
- Si ↔ Se: remembered experience and current observation.

Intuition without observation can become ungrounded; observation without intuition may miss implications. Thinking without valuation may optimize undesirable goals; valuation without reasoning may lack viable means.

## 5. Codifying cognition in agents

A computational agent can maintain distinct subsystems corresponding to the eight functions: predictive synthesis (Ni), hypothesis exploration (Ne), logical consistency (Ti), empirical testing and planning (Te), preference and policy models (Fi), multi-agent coordination (Fe), experiential memory (Si), and sensors or event observation (Se).

These subsystems may operate concurrently, recursively, or selectively. They are engineering analogies, not evidence that the mind literally consists of eight discrete modules or that an artificial agent has subjective consciousness.

A useful agent model comprises **identity, state, cognition, agency, and relationships**. An environment comprises **state, entities, relationships, rules, and possible embedded agents**.

## 6. Knowledge provenance

An agent should preserve the distinction between observations, recollections, hypotheses, predictions, deductions, tests, and values. Information records can carry content, source, cognitive operation, confidence, time, and supporting evidence. An inference is not an observation; a preference is not an external fact.

## 7. Recursive environments and collective agency

Environments may contain agents, which may contain subsystems, and may themselves participate in larger environments. A community can function as an individual's environment and as a collective agent in relation to another community. An environment should be modeled as an agent only when it exhibits meaningful coordinated action or regulation.

## 8. Four philosophical domains

| Domain | Question | Principal concern |
|---|---|---|
| Ontology | What exists? | Entities, states, relations, environments |
| Epistemology | What can be known? | Observation, inference, memory, validation |
| Axiology | What matters? | Preferences, values, ethics, significance |
| Praxeology | What can be done? | Agency, decisions, action, consequences |

## 9. Foundational principles

1. Entities possess states and properties.
2. Entities participate in relationships.
3. Relationships occur within environments.
4. Some entities possess agency.
5. Cognitive agents acquire information about themselves and their environments.
6. Agents interpret information and form models.
7. Agents evaluate significance through internal and relational considerations.
8. Agency translates decisions into consequential behavior.
9. Feedback enables adaptation.
10. Agents and environments can be nested recursively.

## 10. Unified definition

> Relational reality is the evolving system of entities, states, and relationships through which agents exist, experience, interpret, value, and influence their environments. Cognition encompasses the processes through which an agent observes actuality, perceives possibility, establishes understanding, and determines significance, with each process oriented toward both the agent itself and the environment in which it participates. Agency is the capacity through which these cognitive processes may become consequential to reality.

## Intellectual context and limitations

This is a proposed synthesis informed by Jung's *Psychological Types* (1921), Gibson's *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception* (1979), Varela, Thompson, and Rosch's *The Embodied Mind* (1991), and Sutton and Barto's *Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction* (2018). The eight-function taxonomy is an interpretive design vocabulary, not an empirically established exhaustive theory of cognition. Objective reality is not reducible to belief, and a simulated value function does not establish subjective experience.
